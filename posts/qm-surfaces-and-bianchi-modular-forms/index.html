QM surfaces and Bianchi modular forms
Posted on 2026-11-25

Let F be an imaginary quadratic field. It was recently claimed in the OpenAI slopdrop that elliptic curves over imaginary quadratic fields are modular. It seems like this proof will probably hold up (at least, a few experts have come out and said it probably will).

There are a few wrinkles when one tries to generalise the usual modularity of elliptic curves over \Q to an imaginary quadratic field. The first is that an elliptic curve E/F can have CM by F itself, in which case the Galois representation splits as a sum of Hecke characters, and the associated Bianchi form will be Eisenstein rather than cuspidal. This is quite a specific degenerate case, though, and if E has CM by any field other than F, or no CM at all, we still expect to get a cusp form. The second wrinkle is that, very occasionally, there is no elliptic curve associated to a given cusp form. Instead, the expected geometric object is an abelian surface with quaternionic multiplication: its endomorphism ring is given by an order in a quaternion algebra over \Q.

This possibility was first pointed out by Deligne (per Ciaran Schembri's thesis). The first examples were (twists of) base-change forms, found by Cremona.Abelian varieties with extra twist, cusp forms, and elliptic curves over imaginary quadratic fields. J. London Math. Soc. (2), 45(3):404–416, 1992. For a while it was unknown whether this could occur for genuine (i.e. non-base-change) Bianchi forms; it was Schembri who found the first examples, three Bianchi forms over \Q(\sqrt{-3}) and one over \Q(\sqrt{-1}), none of them base change (even up to twist), associated to genuine QM abelian surfaces. This is Theorem 3.3.1 in his thesis / Theorem 1.1 in the associated paper.Examples of genuine QM abelian surfaces which are modular, Res. Number Theory (2019) 5:11

The correspondence can be described via the L-function. In the elliptic curve case, the expected relationship between form and curve is L(f,s) = L(E,s). When f has an associated QM surface A, the correct modularity relationship is L(f,s)^2 = L(A,s).

I have wondered about this phenomenon since the start of my PhD. Early on, I learned about the classical results of Shimura that constructs an elliptic curve from a cusp form f by integrating it over its level group \Gamma_0(N) to get a period lattice \Lambda_f; the quotient \C / \Lambda_f is then an elliptic curve E_f(\C), and in particular, a curve modular by f. There is no similar method to do this in the imaginary quadratic case; a Bianchi cusp form has realisations as a differential i-form for i=1,2, and the period lattices associated to both are 1-dimensional real lattices. They can be smooshed together to get a 2-dimensional lattice in \C, but this seems to have no arithmetic meaning. When the Bianchi form f has an associated elliptic curve E_f, the period of the 1-form is (conjecturally) a rational multiple of the covolume of \Lambda_f, multiplied by \sqrt{D}, for D the discriminant of F. This was proved by TrifkovićPeriods of Modular Forms and Imaginary Quadratic Base Change, Canad. Math. Bull. Vol. 53 (3), 2010 pp. 571–576 in the case of a base-change form, but remains open in general. Just from looking at the modularity relation in the case of f having an associated QM surface A, it seems likely that the right statement is that the square of the period of the 1-form is a multiple of the covolume of the period lattice of A, although as far as I am aware this is not written down anywhere.

It does not seem like there is a way to reconstruct E_f from the periods of f. I find this funny really, as the Shimura period lattice construction has been around since the 60s, and it took until Wiles et. al. in the 90s/00s to prove that one could go in the other direction. And these curve → form methods seem to be the more robust ones, since nowadays all kinds of theorems about modularity of elliptic curves are known, but very few geometric constructions of period lattices exist. I would be very happy if someone figured out how to do this in the Bianchi case (and have spent a lot of time thinking about it myself).

Somehow, I feel that the generic construction one is after is a QM surface A satisfying L(f,s)^2 = L(A,s) in all cases. It is just that, in the overwhelming majority of cases, the QM surface is E \times E, with endomorphism ring M_2(\Z) (a split quaternion algebra). The L-function relation is then L(f,s)^2 = L(E,s)^2, from which follows the usual one.Editor's note: on rereading this, I believe it should be essentially correct, maybe up to isogeny, in the case where E has no CM. But when E has CM by some other imaginary quadratic field, the endomorphism ring of E is already an order \mathcal{O}, so I would expect the endomorphism ring of E \times E to be M_2(\mathcal{O}). And this is not going to be a quaternion algebra over \Q! I am not sure how to reconcile this.

This is essentially the content of Conjecture 3 in Taylor's 1994 ICM paper,Representations of Galois Groups Associated to Modular Forms, Proceedings of the International Congress of Mathematicians, Zurich, Switzerland 1994 albeit phrased a little differently. Taylor (and many others) use the name "fake elliptic curve" to describe the QM surface. I don't really care for this name, so I won't use it.

This uniform description is nice, but it does make it clear that the job of building the correct geometric object out of a Bianchi form is even more difficult than it seemed before. In most cases one only needs to construct an elliptic curve, so one requires two complex periods. Both periods seem to be bound up in the period of the 1-form, and although one could imagine some sufficiently ingenious piece of arithmetic-analytic gymnastics giving a way to "disentangle" these periods, I know of nothing remotely like this actually being possible. Something like this would already be a big boon. But even that is not enough! Because to construct A in the few cases that it is not a square of an elliptic curve, there are two more periods missing, and nowhere obvious to get them from.

Since the associated surface is QM, I would expect you only really need to know the volume of the period lattice (which should be essentially the 1-period), plus a "direction" vector that generates the whole QM lattice, but again, there seems to be essentially no hope of actually doing this in practice.The only idea I had that seemed remotely plausible was to compute the period associated to the 2-form. There is a lot of analogy with the classical case that makes this an attractive idea, but in practice it seems not to help. I am reliably informed by people much cleverer than myself that there are good reasons to expect this period should be associated to regulators of motivic cohomology classes in degree 3, and for Beillison-type reasons this should not be related to the periods of the underlying curve / surface. I am currently writing a paper about computing 2-periods, so watch this space (and please don't prompt an AI to do it before I can)

Given an individual Bianchi cusp form of level \mathfrak{n}, it is possible to find and certify the modularity of an associated elliptic curve, assuming there is one, although the algorithm is a little ignorant, compared to the direct construction of Shimura. One writes down all elliptic curves of conductor \mathfrak{n}, checks which one has the right traces of Frobenius, then uses Faltings-Serre-Livné to match the Galois representations. The state-of-the-art in the QM surface case is what Schembri does, which is to write down a Shimura curve associated to the order \mathcal{O} in the suspected quaternion algebra B, use a Baba-Granath model to relate points on the curve to surfaces with QM by \mathcal{O}, and search for points that give a surface with the right traces of Frobenius. The exact details can be found in Chapter 2 of Schembri's thesis.

At the previous LuCaNT, John Cremona gave a talk on the status of elliptic curve data in the LMFDB, and shared with me afterwards a list of labels of all Bianchi forms currently in the LMFDB with no associated elliptic curve. This list mostly sat on my laptop, gathering dust, until I came back around to thinking about geometrically constructing QM surfaces in June this year. I gave the job of finding geometric objects for all of these to a Claude code, mostly out of curiosity to see if it could actually do it. By this time it had become clear AIs were getting quite good at math, and although it needed the occasional poke towards doing things that seem obvious to you and me,For example, trying to reduce the coefficients defining the elliptic and hyperelliptic curves it was working with, instead of just pig-headedly using models with coefficients in the hundreds of digits it mostly did a very good job. This is one of the areas it seems that AI is excelling at in math, where the gory details of a complicated computation have been worked out and written down across some number of sources, and the main work is in writing efficient code, organising the data, and computing examples.While I was writing this post, Claude proved the Riemann hypothesis. So it would appear that "important and longstanding conjectures" is another area where AIs are excelling at in math.

Most of the forms have no associated elliptic curve because Magma cannot find the curve with its EllipticCurveSearch functionality,A previous version of this post claimed the lack of curve is "only because the LMFDB doesn't contain data up to that bound", which is not correct. We thank John Cremona for pointing this out. Claude found the missing curves using the p-adic uniformisation method of Guitart-Masdeu-ŞengünUniformization of modular elliptic curves via p-adic periods, Guitart-Masdeu-Şengün, Journal of Algebra, Volume 445, 2016, Pages 458-502 and Claude dilligently found curves in each case. A handful more forms are twists of base-change, and their associated surfaces can be computed by finding the surface associated to the classical form and extending scalars. In the end, there was only one Bianchi form (up to conjugation of the level) associated to a QM abelian surface not arising from base-change, the form with label 2.0.19.1-1849.1-a. Per Claude, writing a = \frac{1+\sqrt{-19}}{2} and F = \Q(a), the Jacobian of the hyperelliptic curve

C: y^2 = (3 + 5a)x^6 + (68 - 24a)x^5 + (- 148 - 56a )x^4 + (136a - 224)x^3 + (64a + 240)x^2 + (116 -60a )x +( - 20- 12a)

is a QM abelian surface over F, with QM discriminant 6, and appears to be modular by 1849.1-a. I say "appears to be" because I haven't gone through the process of proving it using Faltings-Serre-Linvé; this is a service Claude offered, of course, but given I don't really understand how that method actually works (in my head it's just a big magic button that says "prove modularity"), I'd rather not claim an AI proof is correct when I haven't verified it myself.Although AIs are getting pretty good at basically all math, so whatever Claude would supply if someone asked it is almost certainly going to be right In any case, computing random Euler factors for this curve is pretty convincing to me that the Jacobian of this curve really is the right object.

Claude didn't do anything new to find this curve. Its ability to perform tedious searches is second to none, and it seems to be very adept at all kinds of methods for finding the right parameters on the Baba-Granath curves, which I won't pretend to understand. It did do something I hadn't expected when I gave it the list, which was to filter the forms by their possible QM before searching surfaces. I wasn't aware this was possible, but the arguments it used are quite simple, so I assume they must be munged from some textbook or paper in its training data.

We use the general fact that, if B is a quaternion algebra, L a number field splitting B, and \ell is a rational prime such that there is a place w \mid \ell of L with L_w \simeq \Q_\ell, then \ell \nmid D_B, the discriminant of B. The argument goes like this. We have an embedding L \hookrightarrow \Q_\ell, and the isomorphism B \otimes_{\Q} L \simeq M_2(L). Tensoring this over L with \Q_\ell, we get (B \otimes_{\Q} L) \otimes_L \Q_{\ell} \simeq B \otimes_{\Q} \Q_\ell on the left, and M_2(\Q_\ell) on the right.

The first number field one can plug into this is L=F, since F splits B.This follows from considering the endomorphisms of \mathrm{Lie}(A) as an F-vector space, and the fact that the QM endomorphisms are all defined over F. If \ell splits in F, the argument tells us that \ell \nmid D_B, which cuts the list of possible B down considerably. If \mathfrak{p} is a prime of good ordinary reduction for the putative abelian surface A, then L_{\mathfrak{p}} \coloneqq \Q(\sqrt{a_{\mathfrak{p}}^2 - 4N(\mathfrak{p})}) also splits B,This is a more involved calculation, which I would prefer to flesh out another time and so a prime \ell splitting in any of these fields also cannot divide D_B. Running this over the eigenvalues stored in the LMFDB, we are left with possible divisors {2, 3} of D_B, so the search is limited to just the quaternion algebra of discriminant 6. Of course, this is a search over some finite initial set of \ell not split in F, and a finite set of Hecke eigenvalues of f, but in practice it is enough to narrow down the search space considerably. Once one has a list of possible algebras B that could supply the QM, one can search for a surface. Finding a QM surface where the Euler factors match indicates that it is worth performing a Faltings-Serre-Livné computation, and that supplies the proof that the QM really was the one you suspected from the finite search.
