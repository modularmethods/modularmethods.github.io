QM surfaces: coda
Posted on 2026-11-26

In the post QM surfaces and Bianchi modular forms I mentioned a genuine QM abelian surface, found by Claude, which appears to be modular by a Bianchi form over \Q(\sqrt{-19}), as well as talking a little about the relationship between the L-value of the form and the covolume of the period lattice of the associated surface. I am sure that this relationship is instantaneously obvious to those in the know, but since I am in the know of very little, I have worked it out for myself, at least in this case. This is a short account of how you might go about doing that.

To compute the L-value, you need the L-series. Any computational algebra system worth its salt has Dokchitser's methods for computing these to high precisionComputing special values of motivic L-functions. Experimental Mathematics, 13(2):137-149, 2004., for which you need to supply the defining data:

  1. coefficients,
  2. conductor,
  3. weight,
  4. gamma factors.

The Magma handbook describes all of these in detail, and since I'm using Magma to do all of these computations, that's a good place to look to follow along. Apologies to those without the means / Simons to access Magma. I'm sure it all works in Sage, too.

For lots of objects in Magma, you can just call LSeries(X). However, Magma's native ModFrmBianchi type does not currently have such an option, meaning you have to build the L-series by hand. If E is an elliptic curve modular by f, you can get around this by using LSeries(E). But we are working with a QM abelian surface, which is not currently implemented, so we need to specify all the information manually.

We provide the coefficients in the form of Euler factors, which can be obtained directly from the hyperelliptic curve C whose Jacobian is A. Magma will compute Euler factors at almost all primes, just not at (2) and primes over the conductor (norm 43^4). The prime (2) is not an issue, we can obtain the eigenvalue a_{(2)} = 2 directly from the LMFDB. At the prime over 43, since it appears in the level of f twice, the Euler factor is trivial. All other Euler factors are the usual 1 - a_{\mathfrak{p}}x + N(\mathfrak{p})x^2 at the split/ramified primes of F, and 1 - a_{\mathfrak{p}}x^2 + N(\mathfrak{p})x^4 at the inert. These can be obtained from the curve itself, since the Euler factor is always the square of the one defining the form. But computing the whole Euler factor and then square-rooting is overkill, and makes the whole computation take a very long time. The more sensible thing to do is to compute EulerFactor(C,P : Degree := 1), which just gives 2a_{\mathfrak{p}} without doing the unnecessary computation of the rest of the polynomial, then build the factors for oneself.

The conductor is easy enough: it is the norm of the level of the form, multiplied by the square of the discriminant of the field. In this case, that is 1849 * 19^2 = 667489. The weight is the usual 2, and the gamma factors are the usual [ 0, 0, 1, 1 ]. Giving all of this information to Magma, we can define what Magma calls L-series of unknown origin. The critical check when one is doing L-series computations in this experimental way is CheckFunctionalEquation, or the newer CFENew. This checks whether the data provided defines a function satisfying the usual functional equation relation of the completed L-series, with the sign a complex number of absolute value 1. In this case, it all passes fine (after the usual tinkering and getting the formulae wrong), and the resulting L-series has sign 1.

Running LCfRequired(L) on our handmade L-series tells us that we need 11403 terms to reach 15 decimal digits of precision. I left this running overnight, and in the morning was greeted by the value

L(f,1) = 4.65828616566125.

With the abelian surface also defined in Magma, we can create a complex version of it by defining the same hyperelliptic curve over \C (using the embedding from InfinitePlaces), and take the AnalyticJacobian. Then BigPeriodMatrix gives the 4 \times 2 complex period matrix of the surface, which we split into real and imaginary parts to get a 4 \times 4 real matrix M. Finally, we have the ratio

\frac{L(f,1)^2}{|\det(M)|} = 4.21052631578947,

which isn't obviously a rational number. If we had the number to higher precision, Magma's PowerRelation might help, but each five decimal places we ask for from the L-series seems to approximately double the number of coefficients needed. So I just ran it through Wolfram Alpha, which tells us an approximate rational value: we (appear to) have

\frac{L(f,1)^2}{\mathrm{vol}(A)} = \frac{80}{19}.

There are always precision issues when one does calculations like these, so this might not be correct. But the appearance of 19 in the denominator is encouraging. If it is right, this calculation suggests that the period of the 1-form associated to f should satisfy

\Omega_1(f) \sim_{\Q} \sqrt{\abs{D}} \cdot \sqrt{\mathrm{vol}(A)},

with D the discriminant of the base imaginary quadratic field. This is essentially the same as in the elliptic case, where \sqrt{\mathrm{vol(A)}} is \sqrt{\mathrm{vol}(E)^2} = \mathrm{vol}(E).
